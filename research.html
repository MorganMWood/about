Research

My research lies at the intersection of operations management, stochastic modeling, and data-driven decision-making. I study how organizations can use imperfect, high-frequency operational data to better understand, forecast, and manage complex service systems.

Much of my research has focused on airport operations, where passenger flows, limited information, and multiple interacting organizations create challenging operational problems. Working with Raleigh-Durham International Airport (RDU), I have studied how organizations can use imperfect operational data to track populations, forecast passenger arrivals, and improve service-system decisions. These problems are motivated by airports but share fundamental characteristics with many other service systems.

My research has been shaped by collaborations with Fernando Bernstein and Bora Keskin at Duke's Fuqua School of Business, Adam Mersereau at UNC Kenan-Flagler Business School, and Serhan Ziya in UNC Chapel Hill's Department of Statistics and Operations Research.

Research Themes

Data-Driven Service Operations. Developing operational models and decision-support methods that use real-world data to understand and manage complex service systems.

Stochastic Modeling & Analysis. Using probability and stochastic analysis to understand how uncertainty accumulates and affects operational decisions.

Forecasting & Prediction. Combining structural knowledge about an operational system with modern statistical and machine-learning methods to produce useful, interpretable forecasts.

Decision-Making with Imperfect Data. Studying operational settings where measurements are noisy, incomplete, anonymous, or otherwise imperfectly captured, and developing methods that remain useful under these conditions.

Publications

"Data-Driven Population Tracking in Large Service Systems"
with Fernando Bernstein, N. Bora Keskin, Adam Mersereau, and Serhan Ziya
Forthcoming in Management Science · Accepted March 2026

Service organizations increasingly collect operational data through automated sensors, but small measurement errors can accumulate and produce highly inaccurate estimates of the number of people in a system. In this work, we study population tracking when arrival and departure data are noisy.

Motivated by passenger-flow data collected at RDU, we show how measurement errors accumulate over time and develop methods for correcting population estimates. We consider both detection-based resets, which use observed departure patterns to identify near-empty conditions, and costly inspections that provide accurate population counts. The analysis demonstrates how appropriately designed correction mechanisms can substantially reduce the accumulation of error.

More broadly, the work develops methods for operational settings in which information systems produce noisy or imperfectly captured data.

"Show-Up Profiles for Scheduled Services: Estimation and Applications"
with Fernando Bernstein, N. Bora Keskin, Adam Mersereau, and Serhan Ziya
Published online in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management · Accepted January 2026

Many scheduled service systems must forecast when customers will arrive relative to a scheduled appointment or departure. Airports, for example, use "show-up profiles" to estimate when passengers will arrive based on their scheduled flight times. These profiles are useful and interpretable, but are difficult to estimate when arrival data are anonymous and cannot be linked to individual flights.

We develop a maximum likelihood approach for estimating show-up profiles from anonymous arrival data and establish strong consistency of the resulting estimator under mild assumptions. Using passenger arrival data from RDU, we demonstrate how show-up profiles can vary with characteristics such as time of day, flight distance, and whether an airport is a hub.

We also combine the structural information contained in show-up profiles with real-time arrival data and machine-learning methods. The resulting hybrid forecasting approach retains the interpretability of a structural model while adapting to current conditions, producing more accurate short-term forecasts than either approach used alone.

Ongoing Research Collaboration with RDU

A central part of my research has been a long-term collaboration with Raleigh-Durham International Airport (RDU). The collaboration began through the 2021 Triangle Impact Challenge, a research competition launched by RDU and RTI International to identify approaches for improving air travel during the COVID-19 pandemic.

I joined the project as a graduate student when the collaboration was getting underway in 2022 and have since worked closely with the airport and my academic collaborators to translate operational challenges into mathematical and statistical research problems. The collaboration began with passenger-flow data collected in RDU's Terminal 1 TSA security area and has expanded to new operational questions throughout the entire airport.

This partnership is ongoing. Our current work examines restroom utilization, cleaning operations, and passenger experience. RDU has provided restroom-level usage data that can be combined with flight schedules, cleaning records, and passenger feedback. This setting has created several new research questions at the intersection of service operations, forecasting, and data quality.

Forecasting Restroom Usage Under Closures. RDU is undertaking a series of restroom remodeling projects that will require individual restrooms to be temporarily closed. We are developing models that account for substitution between restrooms so that passenger usage can be forecast when some facilities are unavailable.

Cleaning, Passenger Flow, and Service Quality. We are studying how passenger-flow patterns interact with restroom cleaning schedules and how those operational decisions relate to cleanliness and passenger satisfaction. RDU currently collects passenger feedback through QR codes located in its restrooms, providing an opportunity to connect operational activity with measures of customer experience.

Operational Compliance & Data Quality. Restroom cleanings are intended to be logged by janitorial staff, creating a record that can be used to monitor whether operational standards are being followed. In practice, our observations suggest that many cleanings are not recorded. We are studying the implications of incomplete operational records and the resulting challenges for monitoring performance and enforcing cleaning recommendations.

From Airport Operations to Broader Service Systems

Although my research to date has primarily focused on airport operations, the underlying problems extend well beyond airports. Airports are complex service systems in which passenger flows, capacity constraints, multiple service providers, and uncertain information interact to determine the customer experience.

These same characteristics appear in many other service settings. My goal is to continue developing methods for organizations that must make operational decisions using imperfect information, while remaining closely connected to the practical settings that motivate the research.
